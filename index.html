Course

CNT 3004: Computer Network Concepts

Fall 2026, University of Central Florida. Reflections on each assignment, plus the quizzes and discussions.

Homework 2: Network Addressing, Protocols & Routing

Chapters 3 and 4 · Command-line networking tools and an introductory Wireshark lab

Experience
This was the first assignment where I looked at my own computer's network settings instead of just reading about them. I used ipconfig /all, netstat -e, arp -a, and route print to find my IP address, subnet mask, default gateway, and routing table. In the Wireshark portion, I captured an HTTP page load, followed a TCP three-way handshake, compared a DNS lookup over UDP to a TCP connection, and read the TTL values in DNS answers.
Key Skills & Concepts
IPv4 vs. IPv6 addressing, MAC addresses, well-known ports, TCP vs. UDP, ARP, the default route, DHCP and APIPA, time synchronization, and basic Wireshark display filters.
Challenges
My desktop has several network adapters, including virtual ones from VirtualBox, so at first I was reading the wrong adapter's information. I also learned that my ping -6 test failed because my network only had a link-local IPv6 address and no global one, which meant my ISP or router was not providing IPv6.
Takeaway
I now understand how a packet actually leaves my computer: which adapter it uses, how the default gateway gets it off the local network, and how the protocols in each layer show up in a real capture.

Homework 3: Network Media, Throughput & Wireless

Chapters 5 and 6 · Cabling, duplex, bandwidth vs. throughput, and 802.11 frame analysis

Experience
I checked my Ethernet adapter in PowerShell and confirmed it was running at 1 Gbps full duplex through auto-negotiation. For the throughput lab, I captured a large Ubuntu download in Wireshark and calculated my real throughput at about 351 Mbps, or roughly 35% utilization of my 1 Gbps link. I also analyzed a sample Wi-Fi capture to count management, control, and data frames and to measure how often an access point sends beacons.
Key Skills & Concepts
Coax, UTP, and fiber; attenuation and crosstalk; simplex, half-duplex, and full-duplex; multiplexing; bandwidth vs. throughput; CSMA/CD vs. CSMA/CA; SSID and BSSID; WPA2/WPA3; and how management and control frames add overhead to Wi-Fi.
Challenges
Wireshark kept freezing while capturing at gigabit speed because it was trying to display every packet live. I learned to lower the snapshot length, turn off live packet-list updates, set an automatic stop time, and use the command-line tool dumpcap. It was frustrating, but it taught me more about how capture tools work than a smooth run would have.
Takeaway
A 1 Gbps link does not mean 1 Gbps of real transfer. Server limits, TCP ramp-up, and retransmissions all reduce throughput, and I can now measure that gap instead of guessing.

Homework 4: Virtualization, Cloud & Subnetting

Chapters 7 and 8 · Hypervisors, cloud models, remote access, IPv4 classes, VLANs, and HTTP analysis

Experience
I installed Ubuntu in VirtualBox and compared NAT, bridged, and host-only networking. I worked through IPv4 class and subnetting problems by hand, and in Wireshark I looked at conditional GET requests and HTTP basic authentication.
Key Skills & Concepts
Type 1 vs. Type 2 hypervisors, IaaS/PaaS/SaaS, SSH vs. Telnet vs. RDP, how VPNs secure remote access, IPv4 classes and default masks, calculating networks and hosts per class, and VLANs.
Challenges
Firefox kept upgrading my test pages from HTTP to HTTPS, so there was no plain HTTP traffic to capture. I had to find and disable the HTTPS-First settings before the lab would work. I also had to redo some captures because I started them too late or refreshed the page the wrong way.
Takeaway
Seeing a username and password sitting in a packet capture over plain HTTP made the risk of unencrypted protocols real for me. This assignment also connected directly to my homelab, where I run Proxmox, a Type 1 hypervisor.

Homework 5 & 6: Security, Performance & WANs

Chapters 9 through 12 · Risk management, security design, performance monitoring, and wide area networks

Experience
I researched security certifications (CEH, CISM, Security+, CISSP, and CISA) and risk management platforms, then studied firewalls, DMZs, switch port security, and Spanning Tree Protocol. On the hands-on side, I used the Microsoft Management Console, Event Viewer, and Services on Windows, and I used Wireshark's Expert Information and Conversations tools to measure TCP retransmissions in my own download capture and in a sample capture with packet loss.
Key Skills & Concepts
Security policy and risk management, firewall and DMZ design, port security, STP, performance baselines, retransmission analysis, and WAN technologies such as MPLS, SONET, Frame Relay, and satellite.
Challenges
The hardest part was isolating the right TCP stream for my download out of everything else in the capture. Once I filtered to that one conversation, the retransmission numbers made much more sense.
Takeaway
Researching the certification paths gave me a clearer picture of what a career in security looks like. I also learned that troubleshooting performance starts with measuring it, not assuming what the problem is.

Quizzes & Discussions

General reflection

Quizzes
The quizzes kept me honest about the details: Layer 2 vs. Layer 3 (VLANs vs. subnetting), the 802.1Q VLAN tag, trunk vs. access ports, inter-VLAN routing, DHCP relay, VirtualBox network modes, and subnetting for departments of different sizes. Questions that made me work out a subnet by hand were the ones that helped me most.
Discussions
In the IPv6 discussion, I looked at why adoption has been slow when IPv4 addresses ran out years ago. NAT has been a workaround that removed the urgency, and my own homework showed that my network still has no global IPv6. In the IoT security discussion, I wrote about how smart devices often ship with weak defaults and rarely get updates, and why segmenting them away from important systems matters. That idea is the same reason I am setting up VLANs at home.
Takeaway
The discussions pushed me to connect course topics to real-world problems instead of just memorizing definitions.

Overall Reflection

What This Course Gave Me

Coming into CNT 3004, I knew how to plug things in and make them work, but I did not really know why they worked. Over the semester, that changed. I can now read my own network configuration, explain what each value means, subnet an address by hand, and open a packet capture and follow a conversation from the DNS lookup through the TCP handshake to the data transfer.

The biggest improvement was in troubleshooting. Several labs did not go as planned. Wireshark froze at gigabit speed, my browser kept forcing HTTPS, and I had to figure out which adapter or TCP stream I was actually looking at. Working through those problems taught me to slow down, isolate one variable at a time, and check results against real data.

This course also changed how I approach my homelab. Concepts like VLANs, Type 1 hypervisors, network segmentation, and firewalls are no longer just vocabulary to me; they are part of the network I am building at home. Whether I end up in financial technology or cybersecurity, understanding how networks behave and how to secure them is a foundation I will keep using.
